TSMC Seeks Rare‑Earth‑Free Equipment to Reduce China Dependence

2026-10-22 · 3 min read
TSMC is asking suppliers for factory machinery that avoids Chinese rare‑earth magnets, aiming to cut exposure to critical minerals amid tightening export controls.

In a move that could reshape the semiconductor supply chain, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC) has publicly requested that equipment suppliers provide factory machinery that does not rely on rare‑earth magnets sourced from China. The chipmaker’s request comes as export controls tighten and the industry seeks to reduce its exposure to critical minerals that are dominated by Chinese production.

Why the shift matters

Rare‑earth magnets are a key component in many high‑precision tools used in chip fabrication, from lithography scanners to wafer handling systems. China currently controls a large share of the global supply of these magnets, giving it significant leverage over the semiconductor industry. By moving away from Chinese‑derived magnets, TSMC aims to secure a more resilient supply chain and mitigate geopolitical risk.

Supplier response and alternatives

TSMC’s request has prompted suppliers to explore alternative motor designs that use different magnetic materials or even non‑magnetic solutions. Some manufacturers are turning to ferrite magnets, which are less expensive and less dependent on Chinese supply chains, while others are experimenting with advanced electromagnetic designs that reduce or eliminate the need for permanent magnets altogether.

Impact on the industry

TSMC’s leadership in this area could set a precedent for other fabs worldwide. If the chipmaker successfully implements rare‑earth‑free equipment, it may encourage competitors to follow suit, potentially accelerating a broader shift away from China‑dominated critical mineral supplies. This could also spur innovation in motor and sensor technology, as companies invest in research to develop more efficient, magnet‑free alternatives.

Challenges ahead

Transitioning to new equipment is not without hurdles. The performance and reliability of alternative motors must match or exceed current standards, and the cost implications need to be carefully managed. Additionally, the global supply chain for alternative materials must be secured to avoid creating new bottlenecks.

Looking forward

TSMC’s initiative reflects a growing awareness within the semiconductor industry of the strategic importance of critical minerals. As geopolitical tensions rise and export controls become more stringent, companies are increasingly looking for ways to diversify their supply chains. Whether TSMC’s push will lead to a widespread industry shift remains to be seen, but it marks a significant step toward a more resilient and secure semiconductor ecosystem.
