Mars’ 1,000‑mile Cloud Could Unlock New Ice‑Formation Physics

2026-10-24 · 3 min read
A new model suggests Mars may host ice‑forming physics never directly observed before, offering fresh insights into planetary science.

Scientists have unveiled a model that predicts a vast, 1,000‑mile‑wide cloud of water vapor could exist high in Mars’ thin atmosphere. The cloud, if real, would be the largest of its kind in the solar system and could reveal ice‑formation processes that have never been seen on Earth or elsewhere.

What the Model Shows

The research, published in the latest issue of the journal Nature Geoscience, uses data from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and the MAVEN spacecraft to simulate how water vapor behaves in the planet’s upper atmosphere. According to the model, the cloud would form at altitudes around 70 kilometers, where temperatures drop below –100 °C.

Implications for Planetary Science

Because the cloud would be so large, it could create conditions where ice crystals form through a process called “cold‑trap nucleation.” This mechanism has never been directly observed in nature, making the Martian cloud a unique laboratory for studying how ice can form in extremely low‑pressure environments.

Next Steps

Researchers plan to use the upcoming Mars Atmosphere and Volatile EvolutioN (MAVEN) mission’s instruments to search for signatures of the predicted cloud. If detected, the findings could reshape our understanding of Mars’ climate history and the planet’s potential for future human exploration.
