Microsoft Urges FCC to Keep Chinese Optical Gear in Data Centers

2026-11-11 · 3 min read
Microsoft lobbies to prevent a potential ban on Chinese optical transceivers critical for AI data centers, warning it could halt rollout of new facilities.

Microsoft has opened a letter to the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) urging the agency to keep Chinese optical networking gear in the United States. The company argues that a ban on the transceivers would cripple the rollout of new data centers that power AI workloads.

Why Optical Transceivers Matter

Modern data centers rely on optical transceivers to convert electrical signals into light, enabling high‑speed data transfer across fiber networks. These components are essential for the massive bandwidth demands of AI training and inference. Without them, data centers would struggle to meet performance targets.

Microsoft’s Concerns

Microsoft’s letter cites the potential for a “halt” in data‑center expansion. The company notes that the optical gear in question is manufactured by Chinese firms that have a long history of producing reliable, cost‑effective components. It argues that a sudden ban would force U.S. operators to source alternatives at higher cost and with uncertain performance.

Industry Response

Other industry groups have echoed Microsoft’s concerns. The International Telecommunication Industry (ITI) has also lobbied against the proposed restrictions, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a diverse supply chain for optical networking equipment.

Policy Context

The FCC is currently reviewing a proposal that would restrict imports of certain optical transceivers deemed to pose a national security risk. The proposal is part of a broader effort to secure the U.S. data‑center supply chain amid growing geopolitical tensions.

Implications for AI Infrastructure

AI workloads require petabytes of data to be moved quickly between servers. Optical transceivers are a critical link in that chain. A ban could delay the deployment of new AI‑optimized data centers, affecting companies that rely on cloud services for machine learning, analytics, and other high‑performance computing tasks.

Next Steps

Microsoft and its allies are preparing to submit additional evidence to the FCC, highlighting the economic and technological impact of a potential ban. The agency is expected to hold public hearings in the coming months to assess the proposal’s merits.
